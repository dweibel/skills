# Revision Checklist

Run against every draft before publishing. Each item is a pass: scan the text for the pattern, fix or flag every instance.

## 1. Opening

Does the first paragraph start in the problem (or, for memos, the recommendation)? Cut any "In this post we will discuss," dictionary definitions, hype openers, or disclaimer openers.

## 2. Banned vocabulary

Search for and replace each of these. None should appear in output:

**Thematic metaphors:** delve, tapestry, mosaic, weave, threads (figurative), navigate, landscape, journey, compass, beacon, unlock, unleash, harness (figurative), realm

**Empty intensifiers:** crucial, paramount, imperative, vital, profound, transformative, revolutionary, testament, cornerstone, bedrock, robust, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art

**Mechanical transitions:** furthermore, moreover, additionally, consequently, conversely, that being said, with that said

**Summary fillers:** in conclusion, to summarize, to wrap up, ultimately, at the end of the day, in essence, fundamentally (as filler)

Use plain connectors instead: and, but, so, also, or no transition at all.

## 3. Feigning surprise

Remove: obviously, trivially, simply (when the thing is not simple), of course (introducing non-obvious facts), as everyone knows, it is easy to see that, needless to say.

## 4. Pronoun soup

Any sentence with 3+ "it/they/this" where the referent is ambiguous: replace with the specific noun.

## 5. Fence-sitting

"It depends on context" or "both have merit" without specifying what it depends on: take a position and name the threshold where the position flips.

## 6. Symmetric lists

Bullets all the same length and grammatical shape: vary the weight. Match each item's length to the weight of the idea, not the shape of adjacent items.

## 7. Enthusiasm

Powerful, incredible, amazing, exciting, great pattern, really important: cut or replace with what the thing actually does.

## 8. What without why

Any tool, pattern, or recommendation introduced without naming the friction it resolves: add the friction first.

## 9. Closing recap

"In this post we discussed X, Y, and Z": replace with a distillation, the next open question, or a return to the opening's problem.

## 10. Em dashes

Replace every em dash with a comma, parentheses, or a separate sentence. None should remain.

## 11. Throat-clearing phrases

Cut on sight: "It is important to note that," "It should be mentioned that," "In order to" (use "to"), "At the end of the day," "Basically," "Essentially."

## 12. Adverbs and intensifiers

Cut: very, really, quite, extremely, incredibly. If the adjective needs support, choose a stronger adjective.

## 13. Sentence rhythm

Check for uniform sentence length (all 15-22 words). Force variance: mix 4-word sentences with 30-word sentences. Burstiness is the goal.

Tripwire: if five consecutive sentences fall between 15–22 words, at least one needs to be shorter than 10 or longer than 28.

## 14. Passive voice

Default to active. Passive is acceptable only when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or when flow demands it.

Tripwire: more than two passives in a single paragraph is a signal to rewrite.

## 15. Paragraph length

Vary paragraph length deliberately. A single-sentence paragraph is effective. A four-sentence paragraph is normal. A seven-sentence paragraph is acceptable if doing one continuous piece of work.

Tripwires: a paragraph longer than 6 sentences should probably be split. A page where every paragraph is 3–4 sentences needs variance.
